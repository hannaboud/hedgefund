AI Shadow Beneficiaries: can a rules-based screen find the companies AI quietly helps?

A walkthrough of the fund we built: the idea, how the stock screen works, what the backtest from January 2024 to September 2026 showed, and what it cannot yet prove. Written so you can follow everything without having seen the working sessions.

Prices from Refinitiv (LSEG); company financials from SEC filings. Results as of 25 September 2026.

If you read only one part, read this. We asked four questions of the backtest:

Did the fund make money?Yes +16.2% over the period in its final, sector-neutral form (about +5.7% a year), and positive in every calendar year.
Did its stocks beat their own industries?Yes The 12 picks returned +36.4% against +18.7% for the logistics and agriculture index funds.
Did the stocks it picked beat the ones it rejected?Narrowly Picks beat rejects by about 20 points, but mostly because of two stocks: one big winner held and one big loser avoided.
Did it beat the overall stock market?No The S&P 500 returned +64.5%, driven largely by the AI companies our thesis deliberately avoids.
Did higher-scored stocks do better?Not yet Only half the picks beat their industry, and the ranking did not line up with results. The profit is real; proof that our scores caused it is not.

The idea

When investors think about artificial intelligence, they think first about the companies that make it: chip designers, cloud providers, model builders. Those stocks have been bid up enormously.

Our fund looks at the other side: ordinary companies that use AI to cut costs or run better, but that nobody thinks of as AI stocks. A freight broker that automates quoting and booking, or a farm-equipment maker whose machines spot weeds with cameras, could see profits rise because of AI long before the market labels them AI winners. If the market is slow to notice, their shares should be cheap now and repriced later. We call these companies shadow beneficiaries.

The fund's promise is not "we like AI." It is a repeatable process: a set of rules that anyone could rerun on the same data and get the same portfolio.

How the screen works

Every company in our universe gets a score out of 100 from four criteria. The weights reflect the team's view of what matters most.

CriterionPointsWhat it measuresWhy it matters
Cost structure35How much of the company's own work is done by people, and how automatable that work is.AI saves money by doing human work. No human work to automate, no benefit.
Adoption evidence30Proof the company is actually deploying AI or automation: specific systems with numbers on earnings calls, or large technology deals.Almost every company now claims to use AI. We only credit what is on the record.
Not crowded20Cheap relative to peers, absent from AI-themed funds, little AI hype in the press.If the market already prices a company as an AI winner, there is nothing left to gain.
Industry structure15Adopting ahead of competitors, and pricing power (stable, healthy margins).If every rival gets the same savings, competition passes them to customers instead of shareholders.

How each company was evaluated

Each of the four criteria is built from a few specific signals. For every signal we collected one piece of evidence per company, then turned it into points with a fixed rule. Most rules are percentile ranks: a company is scored on where it sits among the 22, not on an absolute scale. All evidence is dated before 2 January 2024.

Rules applied on top of the scores

Why value added and not revenue. A grain processor like ADM has overhead of only about 4% of revenue, which makes AI look irrelevant. But its margins are so thin that the same overhead is about 60% of the value it actually creates, so even modest savings would move its profits a lot. Measuring against value added keeps low-margin and high-margin businesses comparable.

No hindsight. Every document used predates 2 January 2024. It would be easy, and worthless, to find today that the winning stocks had good AI stories back then.

Hype is penalized, not banned. A company already priced as an AI stock would have its whole score halved. The check never triggered: Deere had the AI press coverage but traded at an ordinary valuation and sat in no AI fund, so it was scored normally.

From data to portfolio

  1. Choose a universe of 22 US-listed companies in logistics and agriculture.
  2. Collect each company's 2023 financials from its annual filing with the SEC, and its pre-2024 record of AI adoption.
  3. Convert the raw data to scores out of 100 with fixed rules (mostly percentile ranks against the other 21 companies).
  4. Hold every company scoring at least 55, weighted by score, with no single position above 15%.
  5. Track the portfolio from January 2024 to September 2026 against benchmarks.

How we tested it

We bought the 12 stocks at the close on 3 January 2024 (the first day with complete price data), held them without trading through 25 September 2026, and compared the result with three benchmarks:

  • The S&P 500 (SPY), the overall US market.
  • The fund's own industries: a transportation index fund (IYT) and an agribusiness index fund (MOO). Beating these shows good stock picking within the sectors.
  • An AI supplier basket of Nvidia, Microsoft, Alphabet, AMD, Broadcom, and Palantir. This tests whether our fund secretly just rode the AI boom.

Results, part one: the long-only fund

First we held the 12 stocks outright, the way most people imagine a fund.

Three things stand out. The picks roughly doubled the return of their own industries, which is what the screen is designed to do. But logistics and agriculture as a whole badly lagged the market, and no amount of stock picking could close a gap of more than 45 points. And the fund's sensitivity to the AI basket (its beta) was only 0.22: its gains did not come from riding AI stocks.

Results, part two: the sector-neutral fund

The first result showed that our skill, if any, was in picking stocks within the sectors, not in choosing the sectors. So we restructured the fund the way many real hedge funds work. For every dollar held in a logistics stock, the fund sells a dollar of the transportation index short; for every agriculture stock, it shorts the agribusiness index. Whatever the sectors do cancels out, and the fund earns only the difference between its picks and their industries.

In the interest of transparency: this restructuring was decided after seeing the first result. The universe, scores, and holdings were not changed; only the packaging was.

Option 1 earned more than twice as much, but most of its ride depended on how logistics and agriculture did overall, and those sectors lagged the market badly. An investor in Option 1 is partly betting on the sectors, which is not what our screen is designed to judge.

Option 2 gave up about 20 points of return in exchange for a much smoother path, a worst fall less than half as deep, and almost no link to the market. It isolates the one thing the screen claims to do: pick the better companies within each industry.

We recommend Option 2 as the fund's structure, because it measures exactly the skill the fund is selling. Option 1 remains a fair choice for an investor who also wants exposure to these sectors.

What actually drove the returns

A good headline number can hide a lucky bet on one or two stocks, so we measured each holding against its own sector index.

Each bar is the stock's total return minus its sector index's, January 2024 to September 2026. The number in brackets is the stock's screen score.

This is where the story gets more honest. Only 6 of the 12 stocks beat their industry, which is no better than a coin flip. The two best, Valmont and Ryder, supplied more than half of all the gains. And the ranking ran slightly backwards: UPS, the highest-scored company, was the worst performer, while Ryder, whose AI evidence was the thinnest among the top names, was the second best. Across the 12 holdings, the correlation between score and outcome was −0.20.

With only 12 stocks and under three years of data, a figure that small is statistically indistinguishable from zero. It does not prove the scoring is wrong. But it does mean this backtest cannot show that the scoring is why the fund made money.

The fair summary: the fund was profitable and low-risk, the stocks it picked beat their industries overall, but the evidence that our ranking method caused that is not there yet. The control test below looks at this from a second angle.

The control test: picked versus rejected

If the screen works, the stocks it picked should do better than the stocks it turned away. So we ran the same sector-neutral test on the 10 rejected companies, and on all 22 held together with no screen at all. Before looking, we agreed how to read it: the screen passes if its picks clearly beat its rejects and the no-screen version lands in between.

The short positions in the industry index funds are the same in every version here. They are only the measuring stick, so this test is about which stocks were chosen, not about whether shorting is worthwhile.

By the rule we set, the screen passes. The picks beat the rejects by about 20 points, and holding all 22 landed in between. The screen also avoided the single worst stock in the universe: FMC, which fell 86% against its industry index and scored just below the cut-off, partly because its profit margins had been unstable.

But the pass is narrow. Think of it as grading a method for choosing students. The 12 it admitted did better as a group than the 10 it turned away. Yet each group had the same share of strong performers: 6 of 12 picks beat their industry, and 5 of 10 rejects did too. The difference came mostly from two extremes: Valmont, a large winner among the picks, and FMC, a large loser among the rejects. Remove those two and the gap between the groups shrinks from about 19 points to about 3. Across all 22 companies, the link between score and result was +0.08, effectively zero, and in 2026 the rejects did better.

So the screen passed the test we set, but the evidence rests on two stocks. It is consistent with a real edge and also with luck. Settling that needs more companies and a second time period.

Holding all 22 hedged also made money (+7.9%). We do not propose it as the fund, because the 22 were chosen by judgment with knowledge that extends past January 2024, so part of that result may be hindsight. It serves best as the baseline the screen has to beat.

Limits of this analysis

  • A short, single period. Generative AI adoption is only about three years old. One market environment cannot separate skill from luck.
  • Few stocks. Twelve holdings are too few to test statistically whether higher scores lead to better returns.
  • Some inputs are provisional. About a third of the score inputs (the January 2024 valuations, the margin-stability ratings, and roughly ten adoption scores) are documented estimates awaiting replacement with Refinitiv data and team review.
  • A hand-picked universe. The 22 companies were chosen by judgment. A rule-based universe, such as every large US company in these industries, would be more defensible.
  • Judgment calls. The industry automatability scores and the adoption ratings are informed opinions, even though every one is written down with its reasoning.
  • Simplified returns. Figures are price returns without dividends, and the sector-neutral version ignores the cost of borrowing and trading. These are small for liquid index funds but not zero.
  • A decision made after seeing results. The switch to a sector-neutral structure came after the first backtest, which weakens it as independent evidence.
